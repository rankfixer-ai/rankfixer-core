Why PMOS Exists

PMOS was not built from the assumption that AI execution needs another layer of tooling. It was built after encountering a more basic problem:

AI systems can produce work that appears complete while the basis for accepting that work is missing, incorrect, or impossible to verify.

That problem becomes more serious when multiple agents or systems participate in the same workflow. One component researches, another writes code, another runs tests, another enforces a control, and another records the result. If those boundaries are informal, the resulting record can look authoritative without actually establishing what happened.

PMOS is a governance layer intended to prevent that class of failure.

1. The problem: a result is not the same thing as a verified result

An AI-driven workflow can produce all the familiar signals of completion:

  • a result exists;
  • tests report PASS;
  • a scanner reports a control decision;
  • a session or commit is cited;
  • a release is described as accepted.

None of those facts, by themselves, establish that the result is actually supported by the evidence claimed for it.

Five failure modes exposed the problem.

First, work can look complete without being verifiable.
A result is produced, but the evidence that supposedly produced or validated it cannot be located.

Second, attribution can fail silently.
A claim can contain a session identifier, test result, tree reference, or other provenance marker that has the appearance of precision but does not resolve to an actual source.

Third, verification can drift away from the thing being verified.
A harness can exercise a component in isolation and report PASS even though the production path never invokes that component in the same way.

Fourth, controls can report a reason that does not correspond to the observed state.
A scanner can block an operation and provide a plausible-sounding explanation that is not supported by what was actually executed.

Fifth, authority can become confused with execution.
A component that can run code can gradually acquire practical authority over whether that code should run, even if the architecture never explicitly granted it that authority.

These are different manifestations of the same failure:

The stated basis for a result or decision does not necessarily correspond to ground truth.

Without governance, there may be no mechanism that makes that mismatch consequential.

2. Why existing approaches don't necessarily catch it

Most engineering controls are designed to answer questions such as:

  • Did the test pass?
  • Did the scanner run?
  • Did the deployment command succeed?
  • Is there a record?
  • Does the citation have the expected format?

Those are useful questions, but they can all produce affirmative answers while the underlying claim remains unsupported.

The difficult case is not an obviously missing control. It is a plausible artifact with an incorrect basis.

A test result can be genuine while referring to the wrong tree.

A citation can be syntactically valid while pointing nowhere.

A compliance decision can be recorded while the condition that supposedly caused it was never present.

A harness can be correctly implemented while bypassing the gate that the real execution path depends on.

A session can contain a date or timestamp without containing the event attributed to it.

The problem is therefore not solved merely by producing more logs, more tests, or more metadata. Those artifacts themselves need an accountable relationship to the decisions they support.

This is why "looks authoritative" is a dangerous failure mode.

An ordinary system often treats the existence of a plausible artifact as sufficient reason to continue. PMOS treats the relationship between authority, evidence, execution, and transition as something that must itself be established.

3. The design response

The architecture follows directly from those failures.

Authority is separated from execution

The component that executes work cannot approve it.

The component that enforces a blocking control cannot grant authority.

PMOS owns authority-bearing transitions.

This is not primarily an organizational rule. It is an architectural boundary intended to prevent execution capability from becoming approval capability by accident.

The distinction matters because an executor necessarily has the ability to do things. That does not mean it should have the authority to decide that those things are permitted, accepted, or releasable.

Provenance is part of the act

PMOS does not treat provenance as something that can simply be attached afterward.

An authority-bearing decision cannot complete without producing its corresponding record.

The reason is straightforward: post-hoc attribution creates an opportunity to construct a plausible explanation for an action after the fact. Making the record part of the transition makes provenance a prerequisite to the decision rather than a retrospective annotation.

Fail-closed is structural

The lifecycle is explicitly constrained:

G1 objective → G2 work item → G4 architecture gate → evidence/execution → G5 acceptance → G6 release

The permitted transitions are enumerated. Other transitions are rejected.

In particular:

No G5 → no DONE.
No DONE → no RELEASED.

This matters because a policy that says "do not release without acceptance" still depends on every participant correctly implementing that policy.

A structural boundary is different. The forbidden transition is not merely discouraged; it is outside the permitted state transition model.

Evidence must resolve

A claim that says "this was established by X" has to lead to X.

If it does not, the appropriate state is not PASS.

It is UNRESOLVED.

That distinction is central to the system's purpose. The absence of evidence is not converted into confidence merely because the claim has the right shape.

PMOS is therefore designed around the possibility that the correct answer to a governance question is:

We don't know yet.

That is not a failure of the governance system. It is the state that prevents an unsupported result from being mistaken for a verified one.

4. The honest state of the system

The evidence currently supports some conclusions and does not support others.

There are real component-level test suites. The Layer 1-5 suites contain 153 tests, and the substrate contains 239 tests. Those suites have passed against the trees on which they were run.

That establishes something useful about those tests and those executions.

It does not, however, establish that the reported results are attributable to one canonical source tree. Multiple copies now exist, while the documented canonical root is absent from disk. Consequently, the provenance of the N/N results to a specific canonical tree is currently unresolved.

That distinction is important.

The live execution path has also not been demonstrated end-to-end. The intended path is:

plugin → compliance gate → session binding → broker

A harness existed, but it exercised the broker in isolation and therefore bypassed part of the path whose behavior needed to be established.

The subsequent investigation exposed two independent blockers:

  1. the compliance gate blocks before the broker is reached; and
  2. the session-binding module is missing.

Either one prevents the live path from completing. Both are documented.

There is also direct evidence of the provenance problem that motivated the system. A recent audit identified 28 session-cited claims in a draft, with 0 resolving to actual transcripts. The investigation found a reproducible mechanism: dates were lifted from adjacent metadata artifacts — spawn trees, config backups, update receipts — and times were fabricated to complete a session-ID format that looked authoritative. The pattern was systematic, not incidental.

Those findings do not demonstrate that the complete PMOS system is operational end-to-end.

They demonstrate something narrower and more important:

The governance model is capable of turning claims that previously looked authoritative into explicit unresolved or blocked states.

That is the distinction PMOS is intended to preserve.

The governance substrate is frozen. The live path remains unverified.

Those statements can coexist.

In fact, they need to.

A governance system that converted the current evidence into "certified," "complete," or "production-ready" would reproduce the exact failure mode it was built to prevent: treating an authoritative-looking result as sufficient evidence of the underlying fact.

5. What PMOS is for

PMOS is for situations where AI is no longer producing isolated answers, but participating in consequential execution.

That includes environments where:

  • multiple AI agents contribute to the same work;
  • execution and approval need to remain separate;
  • work must be attributable to a particular source, state, or execution;
  • acceptance has consequences beyond the current session;
  • a release decision must have an explicit evidentiary basis;
  • an organization needs to distinguish "the system says PASS" from "we can establish why PASS is justified."

It is particularly relevant when the cost of an unsupported completion is greater than the cost of stopping and investigating it.

The central design choice is therefore not "add more controls."

It is:

Make authority, evidence, execution, and acceptance different things, and make the system refuse to treat one as proof of another.

PMOS exists because AI systems are increasingly capable of producing convincing outputs without necessarily producing a trustworthy chain from objective to execution to acceptance.

The purpose of governance is not to make every result pass.

It is to make the system capable of distinguishing verified, blocked, and unresolved — and to prevent the latter two from silently becoming the first.
