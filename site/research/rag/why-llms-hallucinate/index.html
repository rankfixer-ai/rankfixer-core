Why Your LLM Hallucinates (And How RAG Fixes It)

Why Your LLM Hallucinates (And How RAG Fixes It)

The Hook

Your AI told a customer your return window was 60 days. It's 14. The customer is now quoting your own bot back at you in a dispute.

This is the most expensive thing about LLMs — not that they're wrong, but that they're confidently wrong. The AI doesn't say "I'm not sure." It doesn't say "Let me check." It generates a plausible, well-structured, confidently delivered answer that is completely fabricated.

And the fix isn't a bigger model. It's architecture.

This piece covers why LLMs hallucinate, why fine-tuning doesn't fix it, and what actually does.

What Hallucination Actually Is

Hallucination isn't a bug. It's a feature of how LLMs work.

A Large Language Model is a prediction engine. Given a sequence of tokens (words, roughly), it generates the most statistically likely next token. It doesn't verify facts. It doesn't check sources. It doesn't know what "true" means. It knows what "plausible" looks like.

There are three distinct failure modes, and most articles conflate them:

1. Confabulation

The model fills in gaps with invented content that's consistent with the prompt but unsupported by evidence.

Prompt: "What's our refund policy?" Response: "Customers can return items within 60 days with a receipt." Reality: Your policy says 14 days. The model invented 60 because it sounds reasonable for retail.

2. Fabrication

The model generates content that's entirely fictional — nonexistent APIs, fake case law, made-up product features.

Prompt: "Does Python have a built-in sort_dict() function?" Response: "Yes, sort_dict() was added in Python 3.9 and sorts dictionaries by value." Reality: No such function exists. The model hallucinated a plausible-sounding API.

3. Grounded error

The model has the right context but draws the wrong conclusion from it.

Prompt: "Based on this document, can returns be made after 30 days?" Response: "Yes, returns are accepted within 30 days." Reality: The document says "no returns after 14 days." The model misread it.

Why this matters: Each failure mode has a different fix. Confabulation and fabrication are inherent to LLMs and require architectural solutions. Grounded error can be fixed with better evaluation and retrieval accuracy.

The Four Causes of Hallucination

Most articles list one cause. There are four, and understanding them is the fix.

Cause 1: Training data gaps

The model was trained on public internet data. It doesn't have your internal documents, your product catalog, your support tickets, or your proprietary research. When you ask it about information that isn't public, it doesn't say "I don't know." It guesses.

A 2024 Stanford RegLab study tested legal AI tools on legal research queries. Even purpose-built tools — trained on legal corpora — hallucinated 17–33% of the time. General-purpose GPT-4 hallucinated 43% of the same tasks. [^1]

This cause is about missing data. But not every hallucination has this cause — model capability, prompt ambiguity, and retrieval failure can all produce hallucinations even when the data exists.

Cause 2: Distributional drift

Facts change. Your refund policy updated last month. Your pricing changed yesterday. The model's training cutoff is months or years old. Anything that changed after that cutoff is unknown to the model.

This is why you can't fix hallucination by switching to a newer model. By the time the new model ships, your documents have changed again.

Cause 3: Prompt ambiguity

Vague questions get fabricated answers.

"What's the return window?" → The model has to guess: Which product? Which region? Which policy version?

The more specific the prompt, the less room for fabrication. But you can't control how users phrase their questions. Customers don't write precise technical queries.

Cause 4: Retrieval failure

This is the RAG implementation problem. You set up a RAG pipeline, but it retrieves the wrong chunks. The AI answers from the wrong document — confidently, with a citation, but wrong.

Retrieval failure is the cause you can address without retraining the model. Better chunking, better embeddings, better query expansion — these improve retrieval accuracy and reduce grounded errors.

The key insight: Causes 1–3 are inherent to generation from training data alone. Fine-tuning doesn't fix them. Only architectural solutions — RAG, evaluation, guardrails — address them.

Why Fine-Tuning Doesn't Fix Hallucination

This is the most common mistake: "Can you just fine-tune the model on our data so it stops hallucinating?"

Fine-tuning teaches a model style and format, not factual grounding.

What fine-tuning does: - Adapts tone (more formal, more conversational) - Enforces structure (always output JSON with these fields) - Learns patterns (write like a clinician, write like a lawyer)

What fine-tuning doesn't do: - Give the model access to your live documents - Update facts when your content changes - Reduce fabrication on queries outside the training data

A fine-tuned model will hallucinate in your brand voice. It will confidently deliver wrong answers in the exact tone and format you taught it.

Fine-tuning is valuable — but for a different problem. It's for when you need the AI to sound like your business. It's not for when you need the AI to know your business. [^2]

What Actually Fixes It

There's no single fix. Production systems use four tiers:

Tier 1: Prompt-level fixes

The cheapest starting point. Add instructions like:

  • "Answer only from the provided context"
  • "If the answer isn't in the context, say 'I don't know'"
  • "Do not guess or infer beyond what's explicitly stated"

Limitations: Prompt-level fixes can help, but they're not reliable. Models can still fabricate when under pressure to produce an answer, especially when instructions conflict or context is long. Measurement is the only way to know whether they're working in your system.

Tier 2: RAG (the main fix)

RAG grounds answers in retrieved documents. The AI doesn't rely on its training data — it reads from your actual content.

How RAG fixes each failure mode:

Failure mode How RAG addresses it
Confabulation Answer is grounded in retrieved text, not model memory
Fabrication A well-designed system can use absence of supporting evidence as a reason to abstain — but this behavior must be explicitly implemented and evaluated. It's not automatic.
Grounded error Better retrieval = better context = correct reading

RAG also gives you two things prompt fixes can't:

  1. Source attribution — Every answer cites the document it came from. If the answer is wrong, you can trace it to the source.
  2. Graceful failure — If retrieval returns nothing relevant, the AI says "I don't know" instead of fabricating.

RAG grounds answers in retrieved documents — which can reduce unsupported answers. Whether it does, and by how much, depends on retrieval accuracy, abstention logic, and evaluation.

Tier 3: Evaluation and monitoring

RAG without evaluation is blind. You need to measure:

  • Retrieval accuracy — Is the system finding the right chunks? (Measure: recall@k, precision@k)
  • Answer groundedness — Is the answer actually derived from the retrieved text?
  • Hallucination rate — What percentage of answers contain fabricated content?

Tools like Ragas (open-source), TruLens, and Phoenix (Arize) automate these checks. They run evaluation on every query in development and sample queries in production.

This is the "budget roughly 20–30% of build cost for evaluation" point. It's not optional. It's what separates a demo from a production system.

Tier 4: Guardrails

The final layer. Guardrails catch what RAG and evaluation miss:

  • Confidence thresholds — If the model's retrieval confidence falls below your calibrated threshold, route to a human
  • Human-in-the-loop — High-stakes queries (legal, medical, financial) get human review
  • Fallback responses — When retrieval fails, serve a defined "I don't know" response instead of letting the model guess

Guardrails are the safety net. They assume hallucination will happen and minimize the damage when it does.

The production answer: RAG + evaluation + guardrails. Not one alone.

The Cost of Getting It Wrong

Hallucination isn't a technical problem. It's a business cost.

Customer trust

The refund policy example isn't hypothetical. Companies have had customers quote AI-generated policies back at them. Once a customer learns the AI is wrong, they stop trusting it — and they tell others.

Legal exposure

In regulated industries (legal, healthcare, financial), a hallucinated answer isn't just embarrassing. It's a liability. Air Canada was held liable in 2024 for a chatbot hallucinating a discount policy. [^4]

Support ticket volume

When the AI gives wrong answers, customers open tickets to correct them. The AI was supposed to reduce support load. Instead, it increases it.

Engineering time

Debugging hallucination is expensive. You're tracing wrong answers through retrieval, generation, and evaluation layers. Without proper tooling, it's weeks of work to identify the root cause.

The cost of building a proper RAG system is typically $15K–$60K. The cost of not building one is measured in customer churn, legal exposure, and engineering time. The ROI calculation isn't close.

When RAG Isn't the Answer

Not every hallucination problem is a RAG problem. Sometimes the fix is simpler:

Prompt ambiguity → Fix the prompt

If users are asking vague questions, add clarification prompts. "Which product are you asking about?" is often more effective than rebuilding your retrieval stack.

Wrong model for the task → Use a different model

A 7B parameter model will hallucinate more than a 70B model on complex reasoning tasks. If you're using a small model for a task that requires a bigger one, the fix isn't RAG — it's upgrading the model.

No documents to retrieve from → RAG has nothing to work with

If the hallucination is about general knowledge (not your proprietary data), RAG can't help. The model needs to know world facts, and your documents don't contain them. In this case, the fix is either prompt engineering or accepting the limitation.

The honest take: RAG is powerful, but it's not magic. It fixes hallucination caused by missing information. It doesn't fix hallucination caused by ambiguous prompts, inadequate models, or tasks the AI wasn't designed for.

The Hallucination Fix Matrix

Cause of hallucination Best fix
AI doesn't have your data RAG
Facts changed after training cutoff RAG
Vague user queries Prompt engineering
Wrong chunks retrieved Better retrieval (chunking, embeddings, reranking)
Model ignoring instructions Evaluation + guardrails
Model is too small for the task Use a larger model
No documents exist for the topic Accept the limitation
