What to Look for in a RAG Consultant (And What to Avoid)

What to Look for in a RAG Consultant (And What to Avoid)

The Hook

RAG is in 61% of AI job postings. It's also in the pitch deck of every agency that added "AI" to its services page last quarter.

The result: a market full of people who can talk about RAG — and very few who can show you a system that survives contact with real users. The difference isn't credentials. It's whether they've actually hit the failures.

This is the buyer's guide: eight questions that separate someone who's built a RAG system from someone who's read about one.

The Core Problem

Hiring for RAG is harder than hiring for other technical work:

  • The demos all look the same. Every consultant can show a demo. Demos work on clean data and happy-path queries.
  • The failure modes are invisible until production. Retrieval degradation, document drift, ungrounded answers — none show up in a demo.
  • The vocabulary is new. "RAG," "hybrid search," "re-ranking," "groundedness" — many people use these words without having shipped systems that implement them.
  • The work is not one skill. It's retrieval engineering, prompt design, evaluation, and infrastructure. Most people are strong in one area and weak in others.

The question isn't whether they can build a RAG demo. It's whether they've built one that broke — and fixed it.

The Eight Questions

Each question has what to ask, what a good answer sounds like, and what a bad answer sounds like.

Q1: "Can you walk me through a RAG system you've built end-to-end?"

Good answer: Specific — the corpus size, the chunking strategy, the retrieval pattern, the eval metrics, what broke and how they fixed it. They talk about failures unprompted.

Bad answer: "I've worked on RAG projects." No specifics. Or they describe a tutorial they followed. Or the system is theoretical.

Q2: "How do you handle document updates?"

Good answer: "Incremental ingestion, change detection, re-embedding strategy, versioning." They describe infrastructure.

Bad answer: "We retrain the model." This is wrong. Fine-tuning doesn't handle document updates — it's the whole reason RAG exists.

Q3: "How do you measure retrieval quality?"

Good answer: Names metrics — recall@k, MRR, precision. Describes a labeled test set. Mentions that retrieval is separate from generation quality.

Bad answer: "The answers look good." Or they can't name a metric. Or they conflate retrieval quality with answer quality.

Q4: "What happens when the retrieved chunks don't contain the answer?"

Good answer: "Graceful fallback — 'I don't know,' confidence thresholds, human handoff." They've thought about the boundary case.

Bad answer: "The model figures it out." This is how hallucinations happen. The model will always produce something.

Q5: "How do you evaluate groundedness — whether the answer comes from the retrieved context?"

Good answer: Describes LLM-as-judge, faithfulness checks, or citation verification. Understands the distinction between "correct" and "grounded."

Bad answer: "We check the answers manually." Manual review doesn't scale. Or they don't understand the question.

Q6: "What does your evaluation framework look like after launch?"

Good answer: Continuous monitoring, regression tests, alerts on retrieval degradation. Evaluation as infrastructure, not a phase.

Bad answer: "We test it before launch." That's not an answer — it's the absence of one.

Q7: "What would you need from us to scope this?"

Good answer: Document audit, query analysis, success criteria, access to real data. They want to understand the problem before quoting.

Bad answer: A flat number without seeing anything. Or "we use our standard RAG solution" — there is no standard. Every corpus is different.

Q8: "What's the most common reason RAG projects fail?"

Good answer: No evaluation. Or retrieval that was never tuned. Or scope creep from "internal tool" to "customer-facing" without the corresponding investment. They've seen failure.

Bad answer: "Data quality." That's true but generic — it's the answer someone who hasn't shipped gives. The real answer is about architecture and iteration.

Red Flags

Beyond the questions — the patterns that signal trouble:

Red Flag Why It Matters
No public work No case studies, no GitHub, no writing. For a young field, writing is often the only proof.
Guaranteed results "We can guarantee 95% accuracy." Nobody can — it depends on your corpus.
A single tool for every problem "We use [framework X] for everything." Different problems need different architectures.
No questions about your data If they quote without seeing your documents, they're not scoping.
Fine-tuning as the answer to everything It's one tool, not the only tool.
No mention of evaluation If evaluation isn't in the proposal, it's not in the system.

Green Flags

What to look for:

Green Flag Why It Matters
Specificity Numbers, architectures, failures. The details only come from experience.
Honest limitations "This won't work for X." Consultants who know the boundaries.
Written work Blog posts, case studies, technical writing — evidence of thinking.
Questions about the problem They want to understand before they propose.
A phased plan Prototype first, then production. Not a big-bang commitment.
Evaluation in the proposal It's a line item, not an afterthought.

A Scoping Checklist

What to prepare before hiring — whether it's me or someone else:

Category What to Gather
Documents Sample of real documents, formats, size
Queries 20–50 real questions users ask
Success criteria What does "working" mean? Accuracy? Latency? Citations?
Constraints Privacy, budget, timeline, integration surface
Existing system What's in place now? What's failing?
Stakeholders Who decides? Who uses it?

A consultant who asks for these has done this before. A consultant who doesn't is learning on your budget.

I build AI systems with the same architectural rigor RAG requires. The case study documents what I've built — an adaptive learning engine that surfaces the same decisions a RAG system makes. The field is young. The honest position is: nobody has a decade of RAG experience. What matters is whether someone has shipped something, broken it, and fixed it — and whether they can talk about that honestly.
